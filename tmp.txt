Diffusion models have become the dominant foundation for high-quality visual generation and editing, supporting a wide range of applications including text-to-image synthesis, instruction-based image editing, content creation, personalized generation, and interactive design. Their success is largely driven by the ability to progressively transform noise into structured visual content through a sequence of denoising evaluations. However, state-of-the-art diffusion systems often require tens or even hundreds of network evaluations to produce a single image. This iterative sampling process introduces substantial computational cost and latency, limiting the deployment of large-scale generative models in real-time applications, resource-constrained environments, and high-throughput production systems. Few-step distillation addresses this limitation by compressing the original multi-step generation trajectory into only one to four inference steps. Although recent methods have achieved impressive improvements in sampling speed, aggressive step reduction continues to produce fundamental trade-offs that are not fully understood.

A central challenge is that few-step students often preserve the average perceptual quality of the teacher while losing important properties of the original generative distribution. In particular, distilled models may contract distributional support, generate less diverse outputs for the same prompt, overproduce visually common modes, and fail to represent rare but valid concepts. They may also exhibit weaker compositional reasoning, reduced text rendering accuracy, poorer complex instruction following, and lower fidelity in structure-sensitive image-editing tasks. These problems become more severe when the number of sampling steps is reduced, because a small number of student transitions must approximate the functions performed by a much longer teacher trajectory. Moreover, few-step distillation is unusually sensitive to training data composition, teacher quality, task mixture, timestep scheduling, perceptual rewards, and optimization details. A training recipe that improves one benchmark or data domain may reduce performance on another, while a teacher that is strong in perceptual preference may introduce structural drift or suppress diversity when used without appropriate constraints.
•	Existing studies have made substantial progress by improving distillation objectives, distribution-matching formulations, consistency constraints, adversarial supervision, human-preference optimization, or training efficiency. Nevertheless, these components are commonly studied in isolation. Distillation losses are often designed without explicitly considering how different denoising stages contribute to the final output distribution. Data selection is usually based on static categories, global quality scores, or heuristic mixtures rather than the evolving state of the student. Teacher models are typically treated as uniformly useful across all timesteps, tasks, and examples. Similarly, perceptual reward models are frequently optimized as fixed scalar evaluators, even though they may contain uncertainty, bias, blind spots, and exploitable shortcuts. As a result, current approaches lack a unified account of how sampling stages, data, teachers, and perceptual feedback interact to determine quality, diversity, robustness, and computational efficiency.
•	This project seeks to establish such a unified framework for few-step visual generation. It is motivated by three related hypotheses. First, different denoising stages play complementary rather than interchangeable roles. Earlier stages may be primarily responsible for global structure, semantic layout, and broad distributional support, whereas later stages may contribute more strongly to local texture, visual realism, text details, identity consistency, and perceptual refinement. Compressing these roles into a small number of student transitions without explicitly separating them may cause conflicting gradients and unstable optimization. Second, the usefulness of a training sample, task, reward, or teacher is conditional on the current student state and the corresponding distillation stage. A sample that is informative during early training may become redundant later, while a specialized teacher may be beneficial only for particular tasks or late-stage refinements. Third, relative, multi-dimensional, and uncertainty-aware perceptual feedback is safer and more informative than a single absolute quality score. Scalar rewards can obscure trade-offs between realism, alignment, diversity, structure, and editing fidelity, and may encourage the student to exploit systematic weaknesses in the evaluator.

The first research direction will characterize the functional and optimization roles of different denoising stages. The project will analyze step-wise gradients, teacher-student discrepancies, trajectory geometry, feature evolution, and changes in support coverage across the sampling process. These analyses will be used to determine which stages primarily control global distribution preservation and which stages contribute to local perceptual improvement. Based on these findings, the project will develop adaptive role-separated distribution-matching methods. Instead of applying a uniform objective to all student transitions, the proposed framework will assign stage-dependent objectives and constraints, separating support-preserving gradients from local quality-enhancing gradients when necessary. It will also investigate adaptive weighting mechanisms that respond to the current student error, prompt difficulty, trajectory uncertainty, and the degree of compression. The goal is to prevent later-stage perceptual optimization from overriding global semantic structure or collapsing diverse modes inherited from the teacher.

The second research direction will study conditional data, task, and teacher value. Conventional distillation pipelines often rely on fixed datasets and manually selected task ratios, even though different data categories can have highly unequal effects on student generalization. More data does not necessarily lead to better performance, and mixing heterogeneous data may introduce gradient competition. This project will estimate the marginal value of individual samples, semantic categories, generation tasks, editing tasks, and teacher signals under a fixed compute budget. Value estimation will incorporate student uncertainty, gradient novelty, loss reduction, distribution coverage, and transfer across evaluation categories. These signals will support compute-aware curricula that prioritize examples with the greatest expected contribution at each training stage. The project will further develop multi-teacher routing mechanisms in which a stable base teacher preserves structure and broad coverage, while task-specialized or preference-aligned teachers provide targeted guidance where their strengths are most useful. Routing decisions will be conditioned on timestep, task type, sample characteristics, and student state rather than determined by a fixed global mixture.

The third research direction will integrate reasoning-based perceptual feedback into distillation while reducing reward hacking and assessment-optimization asymmetry. Recent multimodal evaluators can assess images along multiple dimensions and provide comparative judgments or natural-language rationales. However, directly optimizing such evaluators may cause the generator to exploit patterns that receive high scores without producing genuinely better images. The project will therefore construct relative and multi-dimensional feedback that separately evaluates visual quality, prompt consistency, editing faithfulness, structural preservation, typography, identity, and diversity. Held-out critics will be used to detect overfitting to the primary reward model, while uncertainty calibration will identify examples for which evaluator judgments are unstable or unreliable. Trust-region constraints, conservative policy updates, disagreement penalties, and periodic evaluation with unseen critics will limit excessive movement toward narrow reward-model preferences. Reasoning traces will be treated as structured diagnostic signals rather than unquestioned supervision, allowing the system to identify the visual attributes responsible for a preference while remaining robust to incorrect explanations.

Evaluation will be conducted using public image-generation and image-editing checkpoints, open datasets, and reproducible training baselines. Experiments will jointly measure perceptual quality, text-prompt alignment, complex instruction following, editing fidelity, structural consistency, identity preservation, support coverage, within-prompt diversity, cross-prompt diversity, training cost, memory usage, sampling latency, and robustness across evaluators. Ablation studies will isolate the contributions of stage separation, adaptive curricula, teacher routing, uncertainty calibration, and trust-region optimization. Particular attention will be given to whether gains persist across model architectures, resolutions, data domains, and different numbers of student sampling steps.




This research addresses a central barrier to the practical deployment of diffusion models: the substantial degradation in diversity, structural fidelity, instruction following, and robustness that often occurs when generation is compressed to only a few inference steps. Rather than treating distillation objectives, training data, teacher guidance, and perceptual feedback as independent components, the project develops a unified framework for understanding and optimizing their interactions. By identifying the complementary roles of different denoising stages, estimating the conditional value of data and teachers, and introducing multi-dimensional, uncertainty-aware perceptual supervision, the research aims to preserve both distributional coverage and visual quality under severe computational constraints. The resulting methods could enable faster, more reliable, and more data-efficient visual generation and editing systems, supporting real-time, resource-constrained, and large-scale applications while providing broader insights into generative model compression, distribution preservation, and safe reward-based optimization.
